Front-Running TWAPs on Hyperliquid

A forced, public and signed order flow. The alpha was real and held out of sample; it then decayed month after month as other wallets learned to trade the same flow.

Brener_0xResearch note No. 01303 days of on-chain fills11 figures + 15 supplementary
Abstract

Hyperliquid publishes every fill with the wallets on both sides, including the child orders of its native TWAP algorithm. From the first slice of a TWAP anyone can see its direction and its wallet, and the order action discloses its size and duration. I ask whether this flow can be front-run from public data alone, at a profit after fees, spread and my own market impact. Over 448,836 TWAPs (41.9 billion dollars, 150 in-sample days) the naive drift after the first slice of a large TWAP is +13.9 bp at 30 minutes, most of it look-ahead bias. What survives is a pressure that scales with the TWAP's weight in global volume, lasts as long as the TWAP runs, and is not momentum. A frozen rule set earned +35.3 bp net per position in sample and was confirmed on five unseen months against a criterion written in advance (t = 4.59). The lower out-of-sample level is not an overfitting gap: the edge was already falling inside the sample (+61.8 bp in October 2025, +22.2 bp in May 2026) and kept falling (+3.0 bp in October 2026). Over the same ten months, the price response of heavy TWAPs at equal weight dropped from about 110–127 bp to 10–12 bp, and the share of signals followed by a same-side taker within two seconds rose from 14 % to 66 %. Taking the other side as a maker does not help either: the spread earned is returned within five minutes, and the followers themselves no longer earn on the flow.

1Context and objective

Front-running, and where the line is

In the regulatory sense, front-running means trading ahead of an order you know about because of a privileged position, typically a broker trading ahead of a client. It is illegal, and what makes it illegal is where the information comes from, not how good it is. On public blockchains the related practice, MEV, has become a race for block-level latency6. Between the two sits a legitimate activity: anticipating a flow that is forced or announced, read from public data, with no privileged access. I add one constraint of my own. I never push the price to trigger a flow (no stop hunting, no liquidation hunting); I only position ahead of a flow that will happen anyway.

Why Hyperliquid

Hyperliquid runs a fully on-chain limit order book. Since August 2025 each fill is published with both counterparties' addresses, and order actions are visible in the blocks. On a traditional exchange the identity behind a flow is the best-protected piece of information; here it is a column in a public table, which makes the venue a good place to ask how much an observable flow is worth.

Why TWAPs

The first approach was to reverse-engineer large automated wallets. I classified 670,872 wallets over 150 days. Some large automated wallets do move the price with their bursts (+10 to +14 bp at five minutes once biases are removed), but following them never cleared fees, for a structural reason: all of the 20 largest trade on Binance's lead. Their flow was catch-up arbitrage of a price formed elsewhere, not information to anticipate, and that approach was closed.

A TWAP has what those wallets lacked. The algorithm has to keep hitting the book every 30 seconds until it is done or cancelled, it trades one side only, always as a taker, and part of its future is public from the start: direction and wallet from the first slice, size and duration from the order action. Knowing what is coming does not require winning a latency race.

Objective
Find a trading rule that uses only information available at decision time, earns money on a heavy TWAP's flow after fees, spread and my own impact, and passes an out-of-sample test whose criterion is written before the data is read.

Four sources of profit were possible at the outset: the pressure during execution; a reversion after the end if the TWAP is uninformed (hedging, treasury moves, unlocks); providing liquidity to the TWAP as a maker, since its side is known; and information, if large TWAPs are informed5 and prices keep going after them. One obstacle was clear from the wallet study: on coins whose price is formed on Binance, arbitrageurs carry any pressure to the global market.

2Data, notation and protocol

Data

The base data are raw node fills (node_fills_by_block): every trade with both wallets, side, price, size and fee, plus a twapId on algorithmic children. The in-sample period is five months, 2025-10, 2025-11, 2026-02, 2026-04 and 2026-05 (150 days). The out-of-sample period, 2026-06 to 2026-10 (153 days), was stored and not read until the model was frozen. In sample, the universe is a fixed list of 170 perps with an external reference, inherited from the earlier wallet study (Section 1), since the 5-second volume bars behind the weight were built for the coins of that study only; out of sample it is every perp with an external reference (Section 9). Global volume comes from Binance 5-minute klines, perpetual and spot. TWAP announcements (size, duration, send time, final status) come from the public twapHistory endpoint. Appendix A.4 lists the sources and the table layout.

Prices and returns

Two price notions are kept apart. For the shape of a price path I use the median price of non-TWAP taker trades in 10-second bars, so that the TWAP is never measured against its own fills. For execution I use prices actually paid: the best level of the first real taker order on my side after the decision time, which includes the spread. With \(s = +1\) for a buying TWAP and \(-1\) for a selling one, the signed move over a horizon \(h\) is

\[ m_h \;=\; s \cdot \ln\!\frac{P_{t_0+h}}{P_{t_0}} \times 10^4 \quad \text{(bp)}, \]

and the net result of a position, with taker fee \(f\) per side and slippage \(c\) on entry and exit, is

\[ \pi \;=\; s \cdot \ln\!\frac{P_{\text{exit}}}{P_{\text{entry}}} \times 10^4 \;-\; 2f \;-\; c_{\text{in}} \;-\; c_{\text{out}}, \qquad f = 4.5 \text{ bp (perps, base tier)}. \]

Weight

The central variable is the weight of a TWAP in its market, computed from what is known at the first slice: the slice notional \(q_1\) over the average volume per 30 seconds in the previous hour, first on Hyperliquid alone, then against global volume.

\[ w \;=\; \frac{q_1}{V^{\text{HL}}_{1h}/120}, \qquad w_g \;=\; \frac{q_1}{\big(V^{\text{HL}}_{1h} + V^{\text{Bin,perp}}_{1h} + V^{\text{Bin,spot}}_{1h}\big)/120}. \]

Inference

Positions on the same day are correlated (several TWAPs on one coin, one wallet sending several orders), so the unit of inference is the day. With \(d_j\) the mean result of day \(j\) and \(D\) days, \(t = \bar d \,/\, (s_d/\sqrt{D})\). Daily results are mildly autocorrelated (first-order autocorrelation 0.10 to 0.23), so for the headline numbers I also report a Newey-West t with a Bartlett kernel over 4 to 14 days and a circular block bootstrap with blocks of 5 to 20 days. Each test had its criterion written before the computation: a threshold in bp, \(t \ge 3\) (the bar suggested for data-mined factors7), a random control within 2 bp of zero, and a result that stays positive without its top contributing coins. Thresholds were taken in the middle of a plateau rather than at a peak. Later filters were chosen on two selection months (2025-10, 2025-11) and checked on three verification months (2026-02, 2026-04, 2026-05). The five out-of-sample months were not touched until the end.

3Hyperliquid TWAPs

A TWAP is sent as an action {"type":"twapOrder","twap":{"a":coin,"b":isBuy,"s":size,"r":reduceOnly,"m":minutes,"t":randomize}}. The exchange then sends one taker child order every 30 seconds for m minutes, each child fill carries the twapId, and a twapCancel action can stop it. It is a plain uniform schedule, the simplest case of the execution problem studied by Almgren and Chriss3. The children appear in the public trade feed with a null transaction hash, which makes them recognisable in real time (Section 8).

In sample there are 67.0 million TWAP fills, 448,836 TWAPs, 14,948 wallets, 474 coins and 41.86 billion dollars of notional. Every TWAP fill is a taker fill, and no TWAP trades both sides. Most are small (median 5,700 dollars over 10 minutes), but the 56,312 TWAPs above 100,000 dollars carry 88 % of the notional, and the 7,831 above one million carry 53 % and account for a median 14 % of their coin's volume while they run. 91 % of the notional goes to coins with an external reference market (BTC 11.8 billion, ETH 5.5, HYPE 4.8, SOL 2.5), where a TWAP weighs very little (median share of volume 0.04 % on BTC). 65 % of TWAPs open a position, and the 20 largest wallets send a third of the notional.

4A first drift, and its biases

The first measurement took TWAPs of at least 100,000 dollars, entered one second after the first slice and read the price at fixed horizons. The criterion was a move of at least 9 bp at 30 minutes (the round-trip taker fee), \(t \ge 3\), and a random control (same coin, same day, random time and side) within 2 bp of zero. It passed easily: +9.7 bp at five minutes and +13.9 bp at 30 minutes (t = 18.6) on coins with an external reference, against −0.5 bp for the control. The drift grew with size and with weight, from +6.7 bp in the lightest quartile to +21.5 bp in the heaviest (Figure S1).

Two biases inflate that number. The size filter is known only once the TWAP is over, and a TWAP cancelled early drops out of the sample. And the entry price is often a bar from before the first slice, so the slice's own impact is counted. I re-ran the test on every TWAP with at least two slices, without a size filter, entering after the second slice, with classes built only from what is known at entry (slice size, the wallet's past TWAPs, the reference class), and with a momentum control.

The biases were real. Without information from the future, the drift on referenced coins falls to +4.8 bp (t = 7.7), and the best class known at entry makes +8.8 bp, still under fees. Two classes out of 24 pass, both on coins without an external reference, and both are carried by a few tiny markets at +90 to +200 bp while large HIP-3 index and commodity markets in the same classes show nothing. At prices actually paid those two classes make +2.1 bp (t = 2.1) and +2.9 bp (t = 0.7), and turn negative without their single best coin (Figure S2). One more fact came out of this step: large TWAPs on referenced coins tend to start after the price has already moved +22.5 bp their way over the previous 30 minutes.

5Weight as the state variable

Weight ordered the first results, but it was measured after the fact. I estimated it at entry (\(w\) above) and built a momentum control matched on the size of the prior move. For each coin and day I draw 40 random times and record the move \(m^-\) over the previous 30 minutes and \(m^+\) over the next 30. For a TWAP whose prior move in its own direction is \(m\), the expected continuation is the mean of \(m^+\,\text{sign}(m^-)\) over random times of the same coin in the same bucket of \(|m^-|\), and the surplus is what the TWAP adds to it:

\[ \text{surplus} \;=\; m_{30} \;-\; \mathbb{E}\big[\, m^+\,\text{sign}(m^-) \;\big|\; \text{coin},\; |m^-| \in B(|m|) \,\big]\cdot \text{sign}(m). \]

On referenced coins the response is monotone in weight: +2.0 bp below 1 %, +4.4 between 1 and 5 %, +6.7 between 5 and 20 %, and +11.9 bp above 20 % (t = 15.6), measured from the second slice; from the first slice the heaviest class makes +16.8 bp. The surplus over matched momentum is +12.1 bp. Momentum explains none of it: on Hyperliquid, a random 20–40 bp move is followed by +0.4 bp on average, and a move above 40 bp by −0.2 bp.

A monotone response to normalised size is what the impact literature leads one to expect. The square-root law puts the impact of a metaorder of size \(Q\), in a market with daily volume \(V\) and volatility \(\sigma\), at roughly \(Y\sigma\sqrt{Q/V}\)1,2. I do not fit it here. The point is only that weight, not raw size, is the relevant normalisation, and that the effect behaves like a dose-response rather than a threshold.

Executable prices leave a thin margin. For weight above 20 % on referenced coins, entry one second after the first slice and exit at 30 minutes give +12.0 bp gross and +2.1 bp net (t = 1.9); entering at the second slice instead loses 1.8 bp. The fee percentile in that protocol included builder fees that some front-ends add and that I would not pay. At the base schedule, perps alone make +5.5 bp (t = 5.1) and spot and HIP-3 markets lose (Figure S3). Since the perp subset was chosen after seeing the result, it was carried forward as exploratory.

6How long the pressure lasts

A 30-minute exit is arbitrary, so I traced the mean path in units of each TWAP's own duration. For perps with weight above 20 %, entering at the first slice, the move is +12.1 bp at a quarter of the duration, +17.2 at half, +22.0 at three quarters and +24.4 bp at the end (t = 30.2), then +21.2 at 1.25 times the duration, +19.1 at twice and +18.7 at three times. The shape is the same for every duration, and longer TWAPs push further: +17.2 bp at the end for TWAPs under 10 minutes, +27.6 for 10 to 30, +34.8 for 30 to 120, and +61.4 above two hours.

Impact that builds while the order executes, peaks when it completes and then relaxes to a permanent level is the profile documented for metaorders. The retained fraction here is about 0.78 of the peak (19.1 over 24.4 at twice the duration), the same order as the two thirds implied by the fair-pricing argument of Farmer et al.4. In practice this sets the exit: the flow stops at the end, and the end lies in the middle of a plateau that runs from 0.75 to 1.25 times the duration.

Two refinements were tested next. Selling back to the TWAP as a maker during its last quarter passed its criterion (+7.0 bp net, t = 9.8), but a plain taker exit right after the last slice, which was on the same pre-declared list, did better at +10.8 bp (t = 12.7): selling early gives up the end of the push, and on a liquid perp the spread earned does not make up for it (Figure S4). Entering at the announcement, about 1.5 seconds before the first slice, added +1.4 bp (t = 18.9), concentrated on short TWAPs (Figures S5 and S6). It also showed that someone was already reading announcements: on heavy perps, 21 % of TWAPs had another wallet taking on my side between the announcement and the first slice, against 11 % in random windows of the same length. I dropped that entry for a practical reason, since the public explorer feed relays only 0.104 % of transactions and catching every announcement needs a non-validator node.

7Who launches it, and where

The wallet

Wallets that hit the API's 2,000-entry history cap, mostly bots, earn much less than the rest, but that cap uses the wallet's whole history, future included. A version known at entry is the number of TWAPs the wallet launched in the previous seven days. The result is a gradient: +33.4 bp for wallets with no recent TWAP, +20.4 for 1 to 3, +22.7 for 4 to 10, +10.1 for 11 to 30, +7.5 for 31 to 100 and +1.3 for 101 to 300. It holds at equal duration and in every month. Keeping wallets with at most 30 TWAPs, in the middle of the 10 to 100 plateau, gives +17.3 bp (t = 10.7) against +5.3 for the others (Figure S7). One reading consistent with this is that an occasional wallet launching a heavy TWAP has a reason the market has not priced yet, while a bot that sends hundreds is expected and absorbed.

The market

BTC, BNB and SOL lose even when a TWAP weighs 20 % of Hyperliquid's volume. In the selected set, Hyperliquid is a median 7 % of combined Hyperliquid and Binance perp volume. Measured against global volume, the picture changes: TWAPs with \(w_g \ge 5\,\%\) make +20.9 bp (t = 16.7), the others −0.5 bp. At equal Hyperliquid weight, Hyperliquid's share of the market decides the outcome: below 10 % there is nothing (+1.9 bp, t = 1.7, for 63 % of the set), and between 25 and 50 % the gain is +20 to +58 bp. This is the wallet study seen from the other side. Arbitrageurs carry the flow to Binance, and the price moves with the TWAP's share of global volume, not of the venue. Combined with the wallet filter, 7,932 TWAPs make +30.0 bp (t = 12.4).

Reasons instead of coin lists

Some coins still lost, and dropping them by hand would have been picking. Instead I wrote down, before computing anything, why a TWAP might fail to move a price, turned each reason into a variable known at entry, set its threshold on the selection months and judged it on the verification months. Two forms of "the market is too big" held up. Adding Binance spot to the global weight removed TWAPs that earned +3.4 bp in verification, against +23.2 for those kept (t = 4.0). Excluding coins whose Binance volume over the previous week exceeded a threshold, frozen at 182.5 million dollars a day, held for every candidate threshold in verification (t = 3.1 to 4.5); it removes BTC, ETH, SOL, BNB, XRP and DOGE. Tokens backed by a real asset were excluded by definition, which in practice meant PAXG. A filter on bot-dominated coins looked strong on the selection months and disappeared in verification (t = 0.25 to 1.04), and a counter-flow filter gave unstable signs; both were rejected.

After the retained reasons, 5,076 TWAPs remain, at +38.3 bp net (t = 10.4) with a median of +7.0 bp. The figure to quote is the verification one, +27.7 bp (t = 8.5), since thresholds chosen on two months flatter those two months.

Two more questions were settled here. The pressure of all active same-side TWAPs on a coin showed a clean dose-response, but for three quarters of TWAPs it equals the TWAP's own weight, and a herd effect from other wallets did not survive verification; it belongs in sizing, if anywhere (Figure S8). And without the announcement the end of a TWAP can be read from the slices themselves, which arrive every 30.0 seconds with a 99th-percentile largest gap of 31.1 seconds, but waiting 35 seconds to confirm it costs 4.4 bp (t = 4.8), because the book refills within seconds once the flow stops (Figure S9).

8From signal to strategy

Entry and exit without look-ahead

Three entries were compared. E1 uses the public trade feed alone, treating a heavy slice with no slice from the same wallet, coin and side in the previous \(G\) seconds as a new TWAP and keeping its own seven-day count. E2 waits for the API to confirm the new TWAP, its size, its duration and the wallet's exact seven-day count, and enters two seconds after the first slice. E3 splits the position between the two. Waiting one more second costs 0.35 bp. The earlier set still contained three filters unknown at entry (at least two slices, an announcement found after the fact, an available exit price); removing them cost about 2 bp. The exit, called A, uses the announced end for completed TWAPs and the cancellation time plus two seconds for cancelled ones, which the API records to the second; exit B is the 35-second rule.

Without look-ahead, E2 with exit A makes +35.3 bp per position (t = 9.8) and exit B +31.0. E2 earns 20 % more per day than E1 (1,451 against 1,211 bp a day at zero size). Much of the gain comes from cancelled TWAPs (+64.5 bp against +26.4 for completed ones): when a large player stops, the price comes back quickly (Figure S10).

Everything this requires is in the free public feed. Every TWAP fill has a null transaction hash, and about 99.4 % of null-hash trades are TWAP slices, so a slice can be recognised as it prints, with the TWAP's wallet as aggressor. A 15-minute live probe measured slices arriving 0.35 seconds after their block and the API returning a new TWAP 1.19 seconds after its first slice (medians). The rule fires about 39 times a day and rarely has more than four positions open (Figures S11 and S12).

My own impact

There is no historical order book for these months, so my slippage needs a proxy. TWAP slices provide one: they are taker orders of imposed size, sent without regard to the book, unlike discretionary takers who take only what is displayed. On 11.8 million slices I measured slippage beyond the best level as a function of \(x\), order size over the coin's volume per 30 seconds, by liquidity tercile. For \(x\) between 1 and 3, one side costs 0.70 bp on illiquid coins, 3.02 bp on mid coins and 6.57 bp on liquid coins, whose books hold many small orders and little depth behind the touch.

Applied to every signal with a fixed size capped at \(x \le 3\), the size-weighted edge stays between 35 and 36 bp from 250 to 20,000 dollars per position, and the daily profit grows with size (216.6 dollars a day at 2,000 per position, 408 at 5,000). Sizing in proportion to the announced TWAP earns more per position but puts 67 to 89 % of the profit in 1 % of positions (t ≤ 3.3), so I rejected it. Size then becomes a capital decision:

\[ N \;=\; \min\!\Big(\frac{r \cdot C}{0.30},\; 3\, V^{\text{HL}}_{30s}\Big), \]

where \(C\) is the capital and \(r\) the fraction of it lost if a position reaches the −30 % isolated-margin liquidation.

The frozen model

The model, which I call Wake, uses the rules above, entry E2, exit A, fixed size capped at \(3\,V_{30s}\), and fees plus modelled slippage on both sides. At 2,000 dollars per position and without a stop it made 27,939 dollars over 129 tradable days (the wallet's seven-day count needs a week of history after each gap in the sample, so the first seven days of each block of months carry no signal), with 83 % of days positive and a largest drawdown of 152 dollars. 52 % of positions win, and the average win is 2.3 times the average loss. The ten best positions account for 11 % of the profit. The two worst came from a real crash, two buying TWAPs launched while a coin fell from 0.54 to 0.10 in six minutes, at about −20 % each (Figure S13).

The second version adds protections set by reasoning before any computation: a −5 % catastrophe stop, isolated margin so that a position can lose at most its margin \(M = 0.30 + 1/(2L_{\max})\), at most five open positions and two per coin, at most ten entries per 30 minutes, and a 24-hour pause after three stops in 24 hours. The result is flat across nearby settings (7.5 to 8.6 % a month for stops from 3 to 10 % and three to eight positions). With 25,000 dollars and 1 % risk per position it made 10.5 % a month over those 129 days in sample (9.1 % if spread over all 150), with a worst month of +3.6 % and a largest drawdown of −0.28 % (Figure S14). Since verification months had earned about half as much as selection months, I wrote down an expectation of roughly half that level out of sample.

9Out of sample

The protocol was written before a single fill of 2026-06 to 2026-10 was read. The criterion was a positive total profit with daily \(t \ge 3\), for the second version with 25,000 dollars, 1 % risk, two positions per coin and exit A, over the 153 days. Nothing was refitted: same impact curve, same terciles, same thresholds, same leverage table. The universe was, if anything, wider (all 179 perps with an external reference).

Verdict
The criterion is met. The model made 3,711 dollars over 153 days, 2.91 % a month, with a daily t of 4.59. The edge per position at zero size is +9.3 bp (t = 5.25), against an in-sample average of +35.3 bp.

The daily t assumes independent days. Allowing for autocorrelation, the Newey-West t of the verdict series is 3.95 with a 4-day window and 3.31 with a 14-day window, so the criterion still holds; the block bootstrap puts the mean daily profit between 11.4 and 38.4 dollars (95 %, 10-day blocks), and none of 20,000 resamples has a mean at or below zero. The same correction takes the in-sample edge from t = 9.8 to between 6.2 and 7.7.

Month by month, the out-of-sample edge reads +13.2, +7.6, +15.4, +6.1 and +3.0 bp. Completed TWAPs kept about half their gain (+26.4 to +14.1 bp), while cancelled ones stopped paying (+64.5 to −2.6 bp) with the same delay in reading the cancellation as before. The wider universe changes nothing (+9.2 bp on the original coins, +9.6 on the new ones). Capacity is small: beyond 25,000 to 50,000 dollars at medium risk the daily t falls below 4, and at 100,000 dollars with 2 % risk it is 1.35 (Figure S15).

Overfitting or decay?

A lower level out of sample is the usual sign of an overfitted backtest8, so the question needs a direct answer. Four observations point to decay instead. The decline begins inside the sample: with the same frozen rules the in-sample months read +61.8, +46.0, +18.4, +32.6 and +22.2 bp, and the out-of-sample months continue that path without a break at the boundary. The three verification months, which selected nothing, had already earned about half as much as the two selection months, in line with the time trend rather than with a fitting penalty. The parameters are not fragile: each threshold sits inside a plateau, each filter is a stated reason rather than a list of coins, and results move little across neighbouring settings. Finally, the decline has a measured cause, described in the next section. An overfitted rule tends to lose its edge at once on new data; this one lost it gradually, as the market learned the flow.

10Why the edge decays

Figure 9 puts two series together: the net edge of the frozen signal month by month, and the wallets that started trading alongside it. The 20 wallets that most often take on the same side within two seconds of a first slice were all absent in the first two months. They appear from February 2026 onward, and the edge falls as their number grows.

I tested four explanations on 12,010 signals over the ten months, measuring the total move from the last trade before the first slice to the exit, and the part taken before my entry price.

Competition fits the time series. The share of signals where another wallet takes on my side within two seconds went from 14.0 % in October 2025 to 66.1 % in October 2026, and their volume from 0.5 to 2.2 times the volume per 30 seconds. By October 2026 the 20 most frequent followers alone touched 53 % of signals. The price response fits as well: in the heaviest quartile of global weight, the total move was +109.8 and +126.7 bp in October and November 2025, and +10.2 and +11.7 bp in September and October 2026, for the same flow.

The two other explanations do not fit. The market did not calm down: the median absolute move over the 30 minutes before a signal was 73.4 bp in October 2025 and 65.9 bp in October 2026, so the edge per unit of that activity fell from 0.84 to 0.05. And the TWAPs did not change: weight, duration, first-slice size and coin volumes are stable, while cancellations rose from 18 % to between 26 and 32 %, far too little to explain a twentyfold drop.

Signals with no follower in the first two seconds decayed as much as the others (+58.9 to +3.1 bp), which suggests that the whole market adapted, through followers who buy ahead of the TWAP and sell to it later, or makers who now expect it and stop stepping back. Speed would not have helped: entering at the last price before the first slice, which needs a node, caps the October 2026 edge at +5.1 bp before spread and impact.

Two series that both trend over a year will always look related, so I tested the competition story where it makes a sharper prediction. If followers were the local cause, the price response should fall most on the coins they follow most, within the same month. The criterion, written before the computation, was a negative coefficient with \(|t| \ge 3\). On 582 coin-month cells (132 coins), with month fixed effects, a control for global weight and errors clustered by coin, the share of followed signals has a coefficient of −5.1 bp (t = −0.4) on the total move; the followers' volume gives t = −1.7, last month's follower share t = −1.5, and signal by signal the sign is even positive (+6.8 bp, t = 2.1). Followers do take part of the move before my entry (+4.8 bp when present, t = 10.6), but the decline is not concentrated where they are. The follower share is the visible sign of a market that adapted as a whole, not a measured local cause.

This is the mechanism Grossman and Stiglitz described9: an inefficiency that is cheap to observe cannot stay profitable once enough agents observe it. Here the move from a 62 bp edge to a 3 bp edge took about a year, and it happened across the whole market rather than only where the most visible competitors traded.

11The other side of the trade

A TWAP still pays on every slice: fees to the venue, the spread to whoever is on the other side, and whatever impact is left. If followers took the pressure, the remaining question was whether the money had moved to the makers. I matched 10.0 million TWAP fills (5.82 billion dollars, two days per month over the ten months) to the maker fill of the same trade and marked each maker position at later non-TWAP trade prices.

The maker earns the spread and then gives it back: +2.28 bp at 30 seconds, −0.45 bp at five minutes and −4.60 bp at the end of the TWAP, because the TWAP keeps pushing against whoever holds the other side. At the lowest fee tier (1.5 bp on perps, 4 on spot), that would leave me at −2.11 bp at five minutes (daily t = −6.0), and between −13 and −46 bp at the end on the heavy TWAPs Wake used to follow. The flow is spread across 8,000 to 11,000 makers a month, the top ten absorb 21 to 30 % of it, and none of them earns consistently. The followers from the previous section are almost never on the other side (0.1 to 1 % of the flow); they trade with the TWAP, not against it.

That leaves the followers themselves. If the edge had changed hands, they should be earning what Wake lost. I took every same-side taker entry within two seconds of a first slice over the ten months (13,013 entries, 2,209 wallets, 25.9 million dollars), priced it at the wallet's own volume-weighted fill, charged the wallet's own fee twice (3.6 bp per side on average), and marked it at the end of the TWAP. Their real exits are unknown, so this is the result of a follower who leaves when the flow stops, the exit Wake used. The 20 most frequent followers made +13.6 bp per dollar (results capped at ±500 bp, t = 2.9), but their median entry loses 1.7 bp and only 49 % of entries win. Over eight months they made 15,400 dollars in total, 10,400 of it from a single wallet, and they were at −3.3 and −1.2 bp in September and October 2026. All followers together made about 68,000 dollars over ten months and lost 9,000 in October 2026. The money did not move to the followers either. Most of what a TWAP pays today goes to the venue as taker fees.

12Conclusion

Following heavy TWAPs on Hyperliquid was a real edge. It was built without information from the future and confirmed on five unseen months against a criterion written in advance. It was not overfitted; it was competed away while I was measuring it. At equal weight the price no longer moves, a same-side taker now appears within two seconds on two thirds of signals, a faster entry would not recover the gap, and taking the other side pays nothing either. The edge was not transferred to anyone: neither the makers facing the TWAP nor the wallets following it collect it consistently today.

Some of what came out of the study applies beyond TWAPs. Most of the first, naive effect was bias: selecting on a size known after the fact and counting the first slice took the drift from +13.9 to +4.8 bp. Flow has to be normalised by the market that forms the price, not by the venue where it trades. The exit followed from the mechanism (the end of the pressure) rather than from optimising a horizon. And a pre-registered pass was not the end of the analysis: the trend of the edge across months said more than its average level.

I keep re-measuring the frozen signal and the follower share each month. If the edge comes back as followers leave, the question reopens; nothing so far suggests it will.

A.3 Multiple testing and limits

The chain above contains 18 pre-registered criteria, plus descriptive variants reported alongside them. Three filters (weight, wallet frequency, global weight) were chosen on the same 150 days, which is where the risk of overfitting sits. Three things limit it: thresholds taken inside plateaus, reasons written before computing, and verification months that selected nothing. The out-of-sample test is the only clean answer, and the verdict rests on it. The \(t \ge 3\) bar follows Harvey, Liu and Zhu7; with 18 tests, a Bonferroni bound at the 5 % level corresponds to \(|t| \approx 3.0\), so the bar is consistent with the number of tests. The daily t treats days as independent; the Newey-West and block-bootstrap checks of Section 9 show that the out-of-sample verdict survives the autocorrelation present in the data.

  • My impact is modelled, not observed. The slippage curve comes from TWAP slices, and I enter two seconds after a heavy slice on the same side, when depth may not have returned. No historical order book exists for these months.
  • Global volume is partial: Binance perp and spot only, without Bybit, OKX, Coinbase or CME, which overstates global weight where those venues matter.
  • API latency was measured over 15 minutes on a quiet evening. The cancellation feed was not tested live, and my own order latency was never measured.
  • Funding is not included; the median holding time is about 10 minutes.
  • The in-sample universe is a fixed list of 170 perps chosen for an earlier study on wallet activity, not on TWAP outcomes. Out of sample the universe was every perp with an external reference, and the edge was the same on the original coins and on the new ones (+9.2 against +9.6 bp).
  • In-sample and out-of-sample months are not contiguous (nothing between 2025-11 and 2026-02, and no 2026-03).

A.4 Reproducibility

All inputs are public. Fills come from Hyperliquid's node data (node_fills_by_block, one record per fill with user, coin, px, sz, side, time, fee, tid, oid, hash, crossed, dir and, for algorithmic children, twapId). TWAP announcements and statuses come from the public info endpoint ({"type":"twapHistory","user":...}). Binance volumes come from the public monthly kline files (5-minute bars, perpetual and spot). The derived tables are one row per TWAP (wallet, twapId, coin, side, first and last slice, slices, notional), 10-second bars of non-TWAP taker prices per coin, and one row per taker order (wallet, oid, time, side, best level, notional, fee) used for prices actually paid. Every number in this note is a function of those tables and of the formulas in Section 2. The code that builds them and prints each number, one script per step in the order of the sections, is in code/twap-front-running, with the signal-level and daily results in its results/ folder.
